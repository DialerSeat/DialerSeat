# RankHonestly — "How to Grow Your SaaS to 250K Users Without Spending on Ads" — working notes

Personal study notes, written in our own words, for applying the book to DialerSeat.
Source: rankhonestly.com/read (Pranav Mailarpawar, 2026 edition). 38 chapters + appendices A–F.
Not a copy of the book; go back to the reader for exact wording and charts.

The case: **ihatepdf**, a browser-only document tool built solo, launched 24 Dec 2025, 249,472 unique users by 17 Sep 2026, zero acquisition spend.
Evidence: Google Search Console (267 days, 111,872 clicks, 3,764,967 impressions, 1,000 queries, 227 URLs), Microsoft Clarity (337,131 sessions; interaction metrics quoted over the 132,748 sessions up to 5 Aug 2026), and a dated ship log.

## Part I — What to build

### Ch 1. Intercept demand, don't create it
- Two ways to win a stranger: persuade someone not looking (needs a budget) or be present when someone already looking searches (needs only existence). Without searched-for demand, nothing else in the book helps — test before building.
- **One-afternoon test:**
  1. Write the literal strings users type (their words, not category jargon). Can't write 10 without inventing → that's your answer.
  2. Confirm humans type them: Google autocomplete (free honest signal, gives phrasing), People Also Ask (adjacent questions), Keyword Planner / free Ahrefs/Semrush for numbers. Phrasing matters more than volume.
  3. Open the top 10 results: either big sites with genuinely good pages (hard, see ch 4) or thin, ad-choked, slow pages (a gift — common on high-volume commercial queries).
  4. **Count the sub-problems** — highest-value hour. "PDF" = merge, split, compress, convert, sign, redact, rotate, unlock, watermark, OCR, extract, compare, organise… each splits again by file type/device/intent. Case found **60 distinct searched jobs**. Difference between a business and a landing page.
- Passion is overrated; a working feedback loop sustains better (month five shipped nothing, traffic +61%).
- **Three failure modes:** (1) writing queries you wish people typed — keep invented phrases on a separate "unverified" list, never build against it; only observed phrases (autocomplete, PAA, forums, support email) count. (2) Adjacent demand — volume you can't satisfy on your page converts at nothing; check "could someone typing this finish their task on my page?" (3) Real demand you can't reach — 100k/mo with 10 strong incumbents is worth zero.
- **Real demand has four properties** (want 4, 3 workable, 2 warning): typed unprompted (autocomplete finishes it); stable phrasing; recurs (case's 20.0% returning users came from documents being a recurring problem, not retention work); intent to act ("how do I…", "[thing] online free") not browse ("what is…") — task intent survives AI synthesis, topic intent doesn't (ch 12).
- **When to ignore this playbook:** category doesn't exist yet; buyer doesn't search (enterprise procurement); high-value, low-frequency problems (that's a sales business).
- Do: write 20 query strings in users' words; check each in autocomplete, drop invented ones; if <10 survive, reconsider before coding.

### Ch 2. The first 90 days
- Monthly clicks: Dec 31 · Jan 346 · Feb 1,131 · Mar 2,052 · Apr 4,409 · May 7,115 · Jun 8,068 · Jul 16,410 · Aug 39,423 · Sep (to 17th) 32,887.
- Why the start looks like nothing: pages not crawled yet (no crawl budget/history; discovery days–weeks per URL, re-crawl longer); pages only provisionally placed, first guess pessimistic, revised with engagement data; payout lag — week-2 work shows up around weeks 10–18. You're measuring the absence of work not yet done.
- Week-by-week: wk 1–2: 32 changes, 31 clicks (only brand). Wk 3–6: 33 changes, 346 clicks (11× on tiny base — misleading percentage growth). Wk 7–10: 12 changes (shipping down two-thirds), 1,131 clicks (tripled) — output detached from input. Wk 11–13: impressions >25k/mo with 4-figure clicks.
- **Watch impressions, not clicks, early.** Feb: 25,058 impressions vs 1,131 clicks = being shown and losing on position (actionable). Impressions in month 2 forecast clicks in month 5.
- Rising impressions + flat clicks = working, early. **Flat impressions after 8 weeks of shipping = something wrong**: not indexed, not rendering without JS (ch 11), or queries nobody searches (ch 1).
- **90-day plan** (after Appendix B 5-day diagnostic):
  - Days 1–7: instrument first — Search Console verified, sitemap submitted, behavioural analytics, activation event defined. Then ship 3 entry points (not 30).
  - Days 8–30: one entry point every 2–3 days. After week one verify: indexed? (GSC → Page indexing), renders without JS?, sitemap read? A non-indexed site produces the same chart as a slow-working one; the difference costs three months.
  - Days 31–60: 8–10 more entry points. **Start link outreach now (month 2, not month 7), two emails a day** — same ~3-month lag. Do the ch 25 directory listings once, never again.
  - Days 61–90: first real export; look at impressions and average position; find pages appearing at all; find high-impression/near-zero-click pages = confirmed demand, month-4 target. **Change nothing** — it's a report on days 1–30.
- Biggest trap: concluding it failed and switching strategy, which resets the clock. Put a calendar entry 4 months out: "do not judge the strategy before this date". Keep shipping meanwhile.

### Ch 3. You cannot pick the winner (key chapter)
- Click distribution over 227 URLs (112,181 clicks): top 1 page 33.9% · top 3 58.7% · top 5 75.2% · top 10 87.9% · top 20 95.6%. 134 URLs <10 clicks in 267 days; 179 <50; 191 <100 (some are duplicates across hostnames from the canonical mistake, ch 7 field 10).
- Shape held as the site nearly tripled (top 5 = 75.4% at 42,320 clicks, 75.2% at 112,181). **Power law is a property of search demand, not a phase. It does not even out.**
- Wrong lesson: "should've built 5 not 60". You couldn't know which 5. Winners: text editor 14,179 clicks, compressor 13,647, text-to-speech 11,955 (TTS looked marginal, built late). Best CTR on site 25.92% at pos 3.98 = handwriting converter added after first edition. The obvious flagship (highest-volume query): 366,840 impressions, 1,724 clicks, 0.47%. Predictions were inverted.
- Right lesson: **make each bet cheap enough that being wrong 90% of the time is survivable**. Optimise cost per attempt, not quality per feature. One tool = 2 hours to 2 days → 60 attempts in 7 months solo; at 3 weeks each it'd be 10 attempts ≈ zero winners.
- How to work: invest in scaffolding (shared components, templates, build pipeline) so attempt 40 is cheaper than attempt 4 (March had 47 changes because it was mechanical). Ship plain; polish only what gathers impressions. **Don't delete losers** — static pages cost nothing and are lottery tickets. Kill only if they carry ongoing cost (maintenance, support, infra).
- Applies only when attempts are independent and cheap; not for one deep workflow. Decomposing into 60 surfaces was a choice.
- Query concentration: top 1 query 30% · top 5 48.9% · top 25 64% · top 100 78.8% · top 500 94.3% of the 1,000 exported. Past top 100 = 21.2%.
- GSC export caps at 1,000 rows; as a site grows the tail gets pushed off the file and *looks* like consolidation — it isn't.
- Head = concentration risk (half the traffic on 5 strings; check your top-5 share deliberately). Tail = what compounding really is: untargeted accidental matches around pages already shipped. Don't chase the tail directly; widen it via depth (ch 7 FAQ blocks/depth sections make pages eligible for unplanned queries).

### Ch 4. Lose the head term on purpose (key chapter)
- Cluster table (9 months): audio conversion (unusual) 102,161 impr / 8,034 clicks / 7.86% / pos 4.89 · colour inversion (unusual) 99,076 / 3,243 / 3.27% / 5.67 · editing 194,097 / 9,711 / 5.00% / 9.96 · compression 453,012 / 6,370 / 1.41% / 8.55 · splitting 177,472 / 1,296 / 0.73% / 7.96 · comparison 54,010 / 328 / 0.61% / 8.10 · organising 269,773 / 532 / 0.20% / 8.63 · **merging (head term) 366,840 / 1,724 / 0.47% / 8.35**.
- Head term climbed from ~pos 12–13 to 8.35, impressions up >100×, still almost no clicks. Eight days after an earlier export it had dropped to pos 35.76 with nothing changed. **A ranking you can't hold is a loan, not an asset.**
- Two obscure conversions: 11,277 clicks at positions 4.89/5.67 — out-earned the flagship 6.5× on 55% of its impressions.
- Why: position is set by competition, not effort. Head term = decade-old incumbents with dedicated pages → you land ~8; bottom of page one converts ~0.47% vs site avg 2.97%. Adjacent low-competition query → incumbents have no page → decent page lands ~5 → 7.9%. **Small query × good position beats big query × bad position.**
- Organise: 269,773 impr, 532 clicks, 0.20% at 8.63 (below PAA, snippet, images). **Impressions are not traffic** (ch 17).
- **Finding unserved queries (an afternoon, no paid tools):**
  1. Autocomplete for shape: category noun + a–z; repeat with "how to", "convert", "to", "from". Look for surprising completions.
  2. Read the biggest competitor's tool list/sitemap for what's *missing* — highest-yield 20 minutes. Their omissions are deliberate revenue-bar decisions, published on their own site.
  3. Harvest verbatim user phrasing from Q&A communities/forums (that's how audio conversion was found).
  4. Search for the failure, not the task: error messages, symptoms ("won't open", "file too large", "corrupted", "removes formatting") — low competition, high intent.
  5. Check supply query by query — **judge the 10th result, not the 1st**: Is there a page specifically about this query (else unserved)? Does it *do* the job or just describe it (doing beats describing almost regardless of authority)? When last touched (stale = undefended)? Intent matched (informational ranking for task query = opening)? How many forums/aggregators/listicles on page one (= engine can't find a direct answer)? If the 10th is a strong, current, task-performing page on a real-authority site → stop.
- How small is too small: 500 searches/mo at pos 4 (~9%) = 45 clicks/mo; the head-term cluster delivers ~200 clicks/mo from ~42k impressions. 4–5 such queries match the biggest term — and you own them. **Portfolio rule: if it takes <2 days to serve and can rank top-5, build it.**
- Rank candidates by volume ÷ competition. Build the head-term page anyway but budget nothing for it and don't measure yourself against it.
- Principle: **at zero authority you can't win on demand, only on lack of supply.**

### Ch 5. What their business model forbids
- Cheaper/faster/better-designed each last ~a quarter. Durable position = **what the incumbent's business model forbids**, not what's expensive for them.
- Case: competitors upload-process-return on servers (that's how they meter, enforce free limits, drive upgrades). ihatepdf never receives the file; verifiable by going offline after load.
- Copying would mean dismantling their own metering/free-tier funnel/pricing. Code is a weekend; the moat is the incentive structure.
- Bigger value than commercial: it gave people **something to say**. Every unsolicited referring domain (curated resource community, tools directory, maker platform weekly feature) cited the architecture, never the tool list. People link to a checkable claim, not "a good merger".
- Where it lives: local-first/offline; peer-to-peer; never collecting the data (huge in health, finance, legal, HR); genuinely no account (26.92% of sessions completed a task with no signup); no lock-in/free export; radical structural price (architecture removes the cost they price against — they'd have to reprice their base).
- **Three tests** (fail any → it's a feature): Could they ship it next month? Would it cost them something they won't lose — name it (metering, email list, switching costs, upsell path)? Can a stranger verify it in one step?
- Trap: a moat nobody wants. Ask whether users choose you *because* of it or tolerate it. Case honestly: users come for free/fast/no-demands; privacy earns links → ranking → users. The structural advantage may not win the user; it gets you written about.

## Part II — The acquisition surface

### Ch 6. Sixty doors, not one
- 94 blog pages → 1,913 clicks (1.71% of traffic; was 2.32% in first edition — blog grew slower than the product). Tool pages → 69,927 clicks. Homepage took the rest.
- **Inversion: every job the product does gets its own page, own URL, own query, works immediately without signup.** Not a features page listing 60 things — 60 pages that each fully do one job.
- Why it beats a blog: the page *is* the product (no hop from reading to doing; 26.92% of sessions ended in a completed download); independence (60 separate competitions — homepage averages pos 9.31 vs winning tool pages 4.0–6.3; broad pages rank badly on specific queries); no decay (job pages stay accurate; winners kept climbing unedited).
- Unit of work: **2 hours to 2 days per entry point**, or the playbook doesn't apply.
- Examples for non-tool products — CRM: /import-contacts-from-csv, /sales-email-templates, /commission-calculator, /pipeline-forecast-template, /email-signature-generator, /meeting-agenda-template. Analytics: /utm-builder, /conversion-rate-calculator, /sample-size-calculator, /ab-test-significance, /page-speed-check, /uptime-checker. Accounting/tax: every form, deadline, calculation, jurisdiction (narrow, high intent, low competition). Design: /png-to-svg, /favicon-generator, /social-image-sizes, /contrast-checker. Dev tools: one formatter/validator/converter per format (risk: near-identical thin pages).
- Rule: smallest complete job for a cold stranger, own URL, no account, repeat.
- Condition: each page must genuinely complete its job — 60 thin pages is a recognised spam pattern.
- **Decomposition method (on paper):** 1) one-sentence whole job; 2) break into verbs (not features/screens); 3) for each verb ask what varies (from/to, for email/print/upload limit — each distinctly-phrased variant = an entry point); 4) cross out anything needing account/team/history/setup (those are features behind the door); 5) count and estimate hours — gates: **20+ entries, average build under 2 days**.
- When not to decompose: pieces worthless alone (one workflow); value is accumulated state/graph (a free calculator beside it is marketing, not a door); each piece needs real infrastructure.
- **Homepage problem:** homepage = 33.9% of clicks (up from 25.6%) but worst-ranking major page (9.31). It earns traffic from brand search. **Homepage is not an entry point; it's the destination for people who know you.** Stop trying to rank it for the category term.

### Ch 7. Build the page backwards (the page spec — longest chapter)
Write fields in this order (not HTML order). The title is a compression of the answer, so it's written last.
- **Field 0 — the query.** One string, exactly as typed, lowercase, mistakes included. One page = one primary query; two queries = two pages. Check: Google it; mixed intents in results = ambiguous, pick a more specific variant.
- **Field 1 — direct answer (write first).** 40–80 words, plain prose, immediately after the H1. Formula: restate the task as a statement + 3–4 concrete steps in one sentence + the one differentiator + the technical reason it's true. Three jobs: featured-snippet candidate, the passage AI engines lift, the 2-second stay/leave read. Mistake: a marketing sentence instead of an answer. Check: read it aloud with the page hidden — could someone do the task?
- **Field 2 — H1.** Query in title case + differentiator. Exactly one H1. Query at the front. Never lead with the brand (except homepage, where brand is the query). Must differ from the title tag (title is for scanners, H1 for people who already clicked). No clever H1s.
- **Field 3 — title tag (highest-leverage string).** Formula: exact query + benefit + objection killed + objection killed. Rules: under 60 chars (~580px); query first; tail kills the reader's fears (cost, watermark, signup, upload); don't repeat the brand on every page (homepage carries brand, tool pages carry the query); write it last. What it cost them: homepage title led with the domain string, not the brand as humans write it; a competitor using the spaced human form took brand searches and sitelinks (ch 9).
  - **Measured value of objection-killing** (searcher's words): queries naming "no sign up/without login/no watermark": 173 queries, 106,660 impr, 6.31% CTR, pos 6.28 · "free": 435, 362,174, 4.13%, 8.52 · no modifier: 425, 1,494,620, 1.40%, 7.97.
  - Within position 6–10: names signup/watermark 5.79% (pos 7.25) · "free" 2.22% (8.59) · no modifier 0.71% (8.25) → **~8× CTR inside the same position band**.
  - Within position 3–6: 6.79% vs 5.32% — the gap nearly closes. **Objection-naming matters most low on page one** (pos ~8), barely at pos ~4: down there it does the job position would otherwise do. Same lever as schema: changes what a ranking is worth, not the ranking.
  - Caveat: these are the searcher's words, not title words; "put objections in your title" is a strong inference, not a controlled test (Appendix C on why nobody runs the clean test).
  - Check: count characters / SERP preview; read yours beside the nine ranking titles; if yours isn't the most obviously relevant, rewrite.
- **Field 4 — meta description.** A pitch against nine others. Formula: what it does in ~6 words + specific mechanics + objections killed + an instruction. Under 155 chars. Doesn't affect rank; heavily affects CTR. Include the query (matched words get bolded). Every clause = a mechanic or an objection. Unique per page (duplicates are the most common easily fixed error). No adjective soup.
- **Field 5 — working product above the fold.** No signup/email gate/trial before it works; no cookie banner covering it on first paint; interactive element visible without scrolling on a 768px-tall laptop. A bounce back to results is the costliest signal. Payoff: 26.92% of sessions completed a task.
- **Field 6 — steps.** 4–6 steps, HowTo markup. Each: imperative verb + exact UI element (real button text) + what happens. Max six (more = product too complex for this page). Repeat the differentiator in the last step (highest attention).
- **Field 7 — FAQ.** 6–8 questions, FAQPage markup. Sources (never invent): People Also Ask (expanding loads more), autocomplete query + a–z, Reddit/Stack Exchange/Quora phrasing, your support inbox. Answer in the first sentence, then 1–2 sentences of specifics.
  - Measured: only **15** question-shaped queries in the 1,000 exported (4,169 impr = 0.2% of non-brand visibility, 239 clicks, 5.73%), however you define "question". The "480 entry points" promise was wrong.
  - But the long tail did arrive: 394 queries of 5+ words, 208,563 impr, 11,673 clicks, 5.60% (~3× non-brand average) — not attributable to FAQ blocks specifically.
  - Keep the field for: pre-chunked Q&A for AI answer engines; it forces objections onto the page (which then feed the title); completeness → accidental long-tail matches. Stop expecting people to type questions. No marketing questions ("why are we the best").
- **Field 8 — depth sections.** 2–4 blocks of 150–250 words. 8a "How to do this without [the standard downside]" — names the incumbents, explains the mechanism, gives a verification the reader can perform (the link-earning section; the only part with an idea). 8b "Common use cases" — concrete noun phrases (bank statements for a loan application, CV + cover letter + portfolio, scanned pages saved separately), each a long-tail match and proof of having done the task. 8c "When to use a different tool instead" — honest redirection; produces helpful-reading internal links (engines tell helpful from manipulative). Best pages run **1,200–2,000 words**; the worst tool page is 758 words. Word count isn't a factor; complete coverage just takes that long.
- **Field 9 — internal links.** 3–5 sibling links per page, in prose, anchor text = the target page's primary query (never "click here"/"this tool"). In-body links beat nav links (nav is on every page, no per-page signal). Link important pages more often. 8c is the natural home.
- **Field 10 — head tags.** Canonical: absolute URL, exact protocol and www/non-www, self-canonical unless deliberately consolidating. og:title/og:description/og:url mirror title/description/canonical. og:image real 1200×630 (no preview = fraction of the clicks). twitter:card summary_large_image + same fields. hreflang: single language → en and x-default both to the canonical (skipping is fine, wrong is not). robots meta: leave alone; shipping noindex to prod is the costliest one-liner. Their bug: four pages canonicalised to non-www while the site served www → split signals. Pick one host, redirect the other, absolute consistent canonicals. **Check: view source on the built output (not dev server) every deploy — 40 seconds.**
- **Build-order checklist:** query → direct answer → H1 → FAQ from PAA → depth sections → HowTo steps → internal links in depth sections → meta description (compress the answer) → title (compress again) → head tags → structured data → view source of the built file. Do it once by hand, then make it a data structure + template (ch 11).

### Ch 8. How to take up more room (structured data)
- Schema does **not** improve ranking (never a factor). It changes how much screen your result takes (stars, FAQ dropdowns, breadcrumbs, step counts). The one lever that works without moving position — key when stuck at ~8 (ch 23).
- All JSON-LD in `<script type="application/ld+json">` in `<head>`; not microdata (generate from the same data as the page; a designer moving a div can't break it). Multiple blocks are normal; they ship six per tool page.
- **Block 1 WebApplication** (or Product / Article / LocalBusiness as fits): name, description, url, applicationCategory, operatingSystem "All", `offers` with price "0" + currency (makes "free" labelling eligible — CTR edge against paid/trial competitors), `isPartOf` WebSite (ties the page to the site entity, helps brand recognition).
- **Block 2 BreadcrumbList** as its **own top-level block** (nested inside WebApplication was valid but largely ignored). Replaces the raw URL with a readable path; one of the cheapest visual upgrades.
- **Block 3 HowTo** generated from Field 6: name, totalTime (e.g. PT2M), tool, steps with position/name/text/url. Hard rule: every marked-up step visible on the page, or you risk losing all rich results at once.
- **Block 4 FAQPage**: Q&A must be visible on the page. FAQ rich results have narrowed — treat any display as a bonus.
- **Block 5 Organization + WebSite — homepage only.** name, `alternateName` (every form people type, e.g. spaced and concatenated), url, logo ImageObject 512×512, **`sameAs`** (profiles you actually control and use: Peerlist, GitHub, dev.to, Medium…). sameAs drives entity recognition → sitelinks. A competitor with an equivalent link profile got sitelinks; they didn't. No lists of abandoned accounts.
- **Block 6 speakable** on WebApplication: cssSelector pointing at `#direct-answer` and `h1`. Costs nothing; measurable effect unclear.
- **AggregateRating: only where real collected ratings exist — enforce it in code** (ratings table keyed by route; emit only if ratingCount > 0 and ratingValue exists; bestRating 5, worstRating 1). They once shipped an invented 4.9 from 8,500 reviews on four pages for weeks (ch 29) — explicit violation, manual-penalty trigger that can strip rich results site-wide. Same for Review blocks and testimonials: named people must exist and have said it — the easiest claim for a sceptic to check.
- **Verify, on built output, in order:** Rich Results Test (authoritative eligibility) → validator.schema.org (stricter on spec) → Search Console Enhancements (what Google actually parsed, site-wide) → view source (JSON-LD is in the served HTML, not injected client-side; ch 11).
- **Four errors behind most failures:** marked-up content not visible (most common, most penalised); relative URLs (every url/item absolute with protocol); JSON syntax errors (one trailing comma voids the block — always JSON.stringify, never hand-written JSON in templates); dateModified left as a placeholder (they use a BUILD_DATE token replaced at build — automate and check).
- Ship five and stop: WebApplication (or equivalent), standalone BreadcrumbList, HowTo, FAQPage, Organization+sameAs on homepage. AggregateRating only with real ratings — for most people, for a long time, not at all.

### Ch 9. Your name is a distribution channel
- Of 74,160 clicks attributable to the top 1,000 queries, **36,567 (49.3%) were brand searches** across 50 spelling variants. Brand CTR **31.55%** vs **1.94%** non-brand (16×; was 5× in first edition — brand CTR doubled while non-brand fell a third because of new low-position visibility, ch 16). Brand: 36,567 clicks / 115,914 impr. Non-brand: 37,593 clicks / 1,936,542 impr.
- Why the name worked ("I Hate PDF"): survives one exposure (retypeable from memory a week later — variants ihatepdf, i hate pdf, ihate pdf, i hatepdf, i hate pdf cv); states the problem the user is already thinking; brand searchers have already decided.
- Brand search = compounding output of everything else (links, forum mentions, satisfied users); closest thing to an owned channel; a competitor can't outrank you on your own name. **Leading indicator of word of mouth.**
- Rules: retypeable after hearing once (say it, have someone spell it back next day; misspellings/dropped vowels tax forever); say what it is/fixes (abstract names need ad budget); **homepage title leads with the brand as humans write it, not the domain string**; claim the entity everywhere; check the name isn't taken in your category before committing.
- Variant table: ihatepdf 22,277 clicks / 31.52% · i hate pdf 8,672 / 25.69% · ihatepdf.cv 1,641 / 70.61% · i hate pdf.cv 685 / 84.57% · ihatepdf cv 616 / 67.62% · i hate pdf cv 427 / 77.78% · ihate pdf 414 / 27.69% · i hatepdf 338 / 39.95% · i hate pdf compress 161 / 72.20%. Variants grew from 27 to 50 as traffic grew.
- Lessons: people reconstruct names imperfectly; the more precisely typed, the higher the conversion (that's navigation, not CTR); **brand + job queries** ("i hate pdf compress", 72.2%) are the most valuable type and can't be targeted — they're a downstream measure of everything working.
- To see whether a Show HN or directory listing did anything, watch brand queries over the following quarter.
- **Entity checklist (10):** homepage title leads with the human-written brand (spaced, normal caps) · Organization schema with alternateName listing every variant people actually type (from your query export) · sameAs for every profile you genuinely use · identical name spelling in every directory listing · same 60-character tagline everywhere · one canonical hostname everywhere · Google Business Profile and Bing Places claimed (entity registrations, not link sources) · WebSite schema with potentialAction (sitelinks search box) · check brand-variant queries monthly for someone else ranking · name not already owned in your category (unfixable later).
- Caveat: part of brand volume is returning users navigating by search (20.0% returning with no accounts). Brand is downstream of ch 1–8; "focus on brand" has the causality backwards.

### Ch 10. How to choose a name you can own
- A near-identical competitor (same concept, near-identical name, different ccTLD) outranks them on the shared brand query and holds the sitelinks — at Domain Rating 1.5 vs their 25 (ch 27). A naming decision, unfixable later.
- **Inversion:** a brand name should be easy to rank first for and impossible to confuse; it should **not** have search volume of its own. Volume in your name = competition, ambiguity, a permanent fight.
- Keyword-in-domain hasn't helped since Google's 2012 exact-match-domain update; it makes you sound like an affiliate site, which costs links. Pages rank; the name is what people type once they know.
- **Three tests (any failure disqualifies):** 1) exact string unclaimed — search as one word and two; an empty SERP is the goal. 2) SERP not polluted — words not owned by a place, language, game, industry term (killed most candidates). 3) Concept not saturated — no glossaries/agency explainers about the phrase.
- Failures (real shortlist, all .com available): *impressiongap* (VAB report "The Impression Gap" + French printing companies in Gap, France) · *eighthresult* (Indian Class 8 board exam results, seasonal) · *provablyranked* ("provably fair" crypto casinos) · *thehonestnumbers* (real company Honest Numbers Solutions + The Honest Company trademarks) · *sixtydoors* (Roblox Doors A-60 monster, Home Depot 60-inch doors) · *earnedsearch*, *ctrgap* (industry vocabulary — would compete with Wikipedia, Adobe, Semrush, Optimizely) · *soberseo* (addiction-recovery meaning dominates).
- The question isn't whether a name has neighbours (all do) but whether the neighbour is **confusable and in your category**. Their winner (RankHonestly) fuzzy-matches to Rankly, a consumer list app — survivable: different category, no trademark conflict.
- **Verify properly:** check availability with **RDAP**, not a registrar search box (a sales funnel; may log searches). 404 = unregistered, 200 = taken. Endpoints: rdap.verisign.com/com/v1/domain/NAME.com, rdap.verisign.com/net/v1/domain/NAME.net, www.registry.google/rdap/domain/NAME.dev (and .app). Shell-loop 60 candidates in a minute; test in bulk rather than protecting favourites. Check GitHub org, publishing-platform and community handles at the same time. Trademark screen: web search clears obvious conflicts; a registry search is the real thing — know which you did. Descriptive names: low infringement risk, weak protection; invented names the reverse.
- TLD pricing (first year → renewal): .com $11.08 → $11.08 · .net $12.52 → $12.52 · .dev $8.75 → $12.87 · .app $8.75 → $14.93 · .org $7.98 → $11.84 · .co $15.76 → $31.20 · .io $28.12 → $51.80 · .ai $82.70 → $82.70. - Buy the defensive spelling fork (e.g. noun vs adverb) and redirect it.

- If you already have a bad name: don't rename once you have brand volume (bar = real legal conflict or a confusable competitor taking your query). Instead: lead homepage title with the human-written brand; alternateName for every typed form incl. one-word and domain; ch 25 Tier 1 entity registrations; buy and redirect confusable variants you can afford; stop varying the string from today.

### Ch 11. Files, not frameworks
- SPA = every route serves the same empty shell; JS rendering by crawlers is queued, delayed, inconsistent — a second-class crawl path.
- Fix was a **build step, not a framework migration**: a script walks the 60 page definitions and writes complete static HTML per route (title, description, headings, body, FAQ, structured data), served from a CDN; the app hydrates on top.
- **Principle: your acquisition surface should be files** — can't be slow, go down or fail to render; cost nothing. Add a build step rather than changing stacks.
- Speed matters more at pos 8 than pos 2: at 8, a slow page sends people back to the results to click someone above you, and that return is a signal.
- Static isn't fast by itself. Field data (132,748 sessions to 5 Aug 2026): LCP 2.488s (borderline), INP 296ms (needs work), CLS 0.126 (needs work), overall 77/100. Static solved the server half; client JS caused INP/CLS (and a 37.16% dead-click rate, ch 19). **Measure real users, not lab tests.**
- **60-second check (highest-value diagnostic in the book):** `view-source:` (not the inspector) on a built page and search for the page's real title (not a shared shell title), its unique meta description, the literal H1, and a sentence from mid-body. An empty `<div id="root">` = every route is a blank shell. Also: disable JS and reload. The failure is silent — pages index as empty shells, impressions stay flat, and you waste months "improving" content nobody read.
- **Four ways out:** 1) build-step generator (theirs; a few hundred lines, full control, content must live in a data structure; toolkit file 06-generate-seo-pages.js). 2) meta-framework (Next.js, Nuxt, SvelteKit, Astro) — best if starting fresh. 3) prerendering middleware — stopgap, monthly fee, crawler/user content drift risk. 4) request-time SSR — correct but heaviest.

### Ch 12. The answer engines
- Referrers to 5 Aug 2026: **8 visits from Gemini, 1 from Claude** — nine sessions. Argued from mechanism only.
- When answers are synthesised, **being cited is the new being ranked**. Informational queries lose clicks; **task intent survives synthesis, topic intent doesn't** (nobody gets a PDF merged by reading a paragraph).
- The ch 7 page is already the right shape: synthesisers want a self-contained, quotable, factually dense passage near the top (Field 1); FAQ = pre-chunked Q&A; HowTo = atomic procedure; speakable points at liftable passages; static HTML matters even more (many AI crawlers run little or no JS).
- **What to do (modest):** 1) make the direct answer self-contained — no "as mentioned above", no backward pronouns; it must make sense lifted out. 2) put a verifiable, specific mechanism claim in it (not "best free tool"). 3) no llms.txt cargo cult — none proven; if one becomes load-bearing it's an afternoon. 4) add AI hosts to the referrers you check monthly. 5) don't chase it at the expense of links (ch 24) — citation is downstream of being a citable source.

### Ch 13. How to be in the training data
- Practice: two posts/day across four platforms for months + daily community participation → 9 AI-assistant sessions. Mechanism sound, result unmeasurable with standard tools.
- When a model names you: user clicks the citation (referral — rarest) / searches your name (brand query, indistinguishable) / types the domain (direct, unattributed) / remembers it weeks later (never attributed). **Referrer count is a floor, not a measurement.** But "can't be measured" is also the seller's perfect argument — build the one measure that works.
- Corpora concentrate in: large community forums, developer publishing platforms, general publishing platforms, code hosts and READMEs, documentation sites, reference wikis — all places **a third party describes you in prose**. You're ingested for being described, not for being useful.
- What was done: 2 posts/day, each answering one specific category question (the same question a tool page answers, written as an explanation); daily community participation; **one consistent, repeated, checkable architectural sentence everywhere**.
- **A model learns an association between a category and a phrase.** Sixty different clever phrasings teach nothing; sixty repetitions of one true, specific, unusual sentence teach one thing.
- **Forums (most valuable, highest failure rate):** new account self-linking → mod removal within the hour, worst case domain added to a site-wide filter (silent, permanent). Do: answer questions without linking (the account is the asset; most contributions unrelated to your revenue); let the description do the work ("I use something that processes these locally in the browser so nothing gets uploaded" — differentiator, category, no link); disclose when you mention your own product (turns a violation into a contribution); the goal is strangers recommending you unprompted months later — make that easy with one memorable checkable sentence.
- **Post format:** lead with the answer (first paragraph extractable and correct alone); specific, falsifiable claims ("runs with the network disconnected" vs "fast"); question-shaped headings in users' words; **one question per post**; include boring specifics (formats, limits, edge cases, failure behaviour) — without them it reads promotional.
- Duplicate content: for search engines set a canonical to your own copy where platforms allow (duplication is a selection problem, not a penalty); for answer engines repetition across domains *is* the mechanism. Canonicalise for search, syndicate for answer engines.
- **The only honest scoreboard — monthly prompt set:** 10–15 questions a real buyer would ask (generic "best way to do X", constrained "…without uploading anything", comparison "alternatives to [incumbent]"); ask every major assistant in a fresh session, same day each month; record: named? position? **what phrase described you?** If the phrase is your architectural sentence, the association took; if generic, fix phrasing, not volume. ~45 cells, 20 minutes/month; moves before traffic does.
- Cost: 1–2 hrs/day ≈ 250 hrs over six months = enough to build the 60 pages; ~5× the ch 28 outreach effort (which produced two editorial links, the only type that moved rankings); ~50× the effort that took them DR 0 → 21.
- For: a training corpus is assembled at a point in time — being early is the whole channel. Against: like the 90 blog posts (2nd-most expensive mistake), it feels productive and goes unchecked.
- **Verdict: do it at half volume, capped at 30 min/day, with the prompt-set measurement from day one, beside link work not instead of it. If you aren't measuring, don't write.**

- Chain, each stage with its own delay, in series: **discovery** (sitemap/internal/external link; days–weeks; the only stage you can meaningfully speed up) → **crawl** (budget by perceived importance; new domains wait longer) → **indexing** (many pages crawled but never indexed; invisible unless you check coverage) → **provisional ranking** (content signals only, usually pessimistic — where founders wrongly conclude failure) → **reassessment** (weeks; engagement data and links; most movement happens here).

Your top-20 queries barely move on the day work lands; it arrives as hundreds of new queries worth a few impressions each, mostly at pos ~8. Watch **impression count and number of distinct queries** during the lag, not average position. Downside: eligibility at pos 8 converts ~1% — tripling it tripled the problem Part IV addresses.
- **Measure your own lag (15 min):** list each page's publish date from deploy log/CMS; from GSC page export find each page's first non-zero-impression date; look at the distribution of differences (not the mean). Add half again for reassessment = the date before which judging is guesswork. Write it down beside the dashboard.

Cliff = technical phenomenon, act now.**
- Danger: abandoning strategy during the lag resets the clock. The month-five stall is structural; nearly everyone hits it.
- **What to do:** watch impressions → then position → then clicks (in that order of movement); overlay shipping frequency on the traffic chart; reduce the unit of work rather than stopping (one entry point a week beats 12 in March and 0 in May); set a 4-month decision horizon in writing; don't count on assets running without you if growth depends on ongoing publishing/community/outreach.

### Ch 15. What noise looks like
- Last 60 days: lowest day 341 clicks, highest 4,608, mean 1,349 — 13-fold range with nothing dramatic happening.
- **Weekday seasonality:** avg daily clicks Mon 549 · Tue 470 · Wed 458 · Thu 391 · Fri 371 · Sat 329 · Sun 366. Weekdays 447 vs weekends 348 (structural 22% gap). Any comparison crossing a weekend is contaminated ("last 3 days vs previous 3" on a Monday → meaningless 30–40% jumps). CTR moves the other way (Wed 2.60%, Sat 3.56%) because weekend searchers skew brand — composition again.
- Other sources: query-level rank wobble (pos 8.4 is served at 6 one day, 11 the next — hundreds of clicks on a 20k-impression query); genuine spikes (links, trends, competitor exits) — rare and indistinguishable from noise on a single day.
- **Rules:** never compare <7 days to <7 days (7/28/90-day windows cancel weekdays); compare like periods (WoW, or same 28 days YoY; never "this week vs last month"); single-day moves are noise; **set an action threshold before looking** (e.g. 20% shift sustained 14 days); look at position before clicks (less volatile).
- **Your own noise band (5 min):** export 60 days of daily clicks; mean ± 2 SD = normal band (~95% of ordinary days). If 2 SD goes below zero the distribution is skewed — a rough guide. A weekly series gives a much narrower band and detects real change sooner.
- **The test saying yes:** 17 Aug 23,967 impressions → 18 Aug 43,422, nothing deployed. Band from prior 4 weeks topped ~38,300/day; 29 of the next 31 days sat above it = a real step, not noise. Position and CTR barely moved → eligibility (ch 14). Cause unknown; no deploy to point to.
- **Small sites are noisier** (relative variation ~1/√count). Below ~100 clicks/day compare nothing shorter than 14 days, prefer 28.
- Longer cycles: annual (work tools sag late Dec and mid-summer; only YoY same-weeks shows it — in year one, don't build explanations for December); **Google core updates** produce real step changes on published dates — check the date before diagnosing a persistent step.
- **Cadence: check weekly, act monthly, judge quarterly.** Daily checks produce actions, which add variables and make next month's data harder to read.

### Ch 16. Falling CTR is what growth looks like
- CTR fell 29.25% (Dec) → 2.45% (Sep) while monthly clicks rose 1,272× (31 → 39,423 in Aug). Dec's 29% was 106 impressions of mostly brand searches.
- Not monotonic: Jul 2.72% → Aug 3.04% with nothing fixed — brand grew faster that month. An aggregate that rises for non-snippet reasons can't tell you about snippets.
- **A falling aggregate CTR during expansion is a composition effect**: new queries enter at poor positions (~1% CTR normal) and dilute the average. Worked example: April's cohort in July 73,282 × 6.02% ≈ 4,412 clicks; new cohort 531,066 × ~2.26% ≈ 11,998; total 16,410, blended 2.72% — old cohort unchanged.
- Danger: the intuitive fix (rewrite titles) hits your biggest, oldest, healthy pages; you won't know for a quarter. They nearly did this in June.
- **Do instead:** never read aggregate CTR while impressions are moving; **segment brand (31.55%, 115,914 impr) vs non-brand (1.94%, 1,936,542 impr)** first; compare CTR only within a fixed position band; use CTR only per page paired with position — pos 3.5 at 1.0% = snippet problem, rewrite title/description (cheapest traffic available); pos 9.0 at 1.0% = normal, needs authority not copy.
- Recurring error: every headline metric is a weighted average of different populations (ch 3 page share, ch 9 brand split, ch 16 position mix, ch 20 device split, ch 21 country split). **The number is never the finding; the decomposition is.**

### Ch 19. What happens after the click
- Sessions to 5 Aug: scrolled 98.58% · dead click 37.16% · completed download 26.92% · returned 19.4% · quick back-click 6.2% · uploaded a file 5.01% · rage-clicked 1.84%. **More sessions had a failed interaction than a successful one.**
- These visitors already found you, chose you over nine competitors and arrived with intent — fully paid-for traffic. **Recovering a fraction of a 37% dead-click rate beats a quarter of link building and takes an afternoon.**
- A dead click is almost always: something that looks interactive and isn't (icon, bold label, card, bordered box, filename); something interactive that's broken (handler threw, unexplained disabled state, load race); works but gives no feedback (2 s of nothing = broken to the user); mis-hit (tiny/crowded mobile tap targets); impatience (clicking during slow ops, counted again). The last three pass every test suite.
- Corroborating: INP 296ms (vs 200ms "good" — long enough to click again → dead/rage/back click); CLS 0.126 (users click where the button *was*); quick back-clicks 6.20%; JS errors 1,942 (top in 474 sessions); rage clicks 1.84%. GSC reported 42,320 clicks for the same window and nothing about what happened next.
- **Fix, cheapest first:** 1) watch ten session recordings (20 min — worth more than the rest); 2) make everything that looks clickable clickable (filename, thumbnail, icon, card → wire to the obvious action); 3) **acknowledge every action within 100ms** (spinner, disabled state, progress bar) — often the fix for INP isn't speed but immediate acknowledgement; 4) reserve space for anything that loads (fixed dimensions on images, canvases, result panels) = CLS fix; 5) read the JS error log against the funnel monthly; 6) re-measure dead clicks, INP and activation — fast feedback loop, no settlement lag.
- Protect the 19.4% returning rate (retention without mechanics; the one number that can't be manufactured) by making sure it works when they return.

### Ch 22. Thirty-one days of zero

- **Cliff = not in the index (technical). Slope = ranked badly.** Telling them apart is the most useful diagnostic act when traffic disappears.
- Diagnosis method (thin content ruled out): nothing shipped in May (last deploy 28 Apr); Google had its record month on the same pages at the same time; cured by a reindex request alone (a quality demotion isn't reversed by asking to be recrawled); returned with broader coverage (2.5× daily impressions). - **Lesson 3 — nobody tells you a channel died.** The blended total hid it (Google was having its best month). **Check each channel separately every week and look for zeros, not declines** — a blended number hides the death of any channel under ~a third of volume.
- **Cliff escalation, cheapest first, stop when one works:** 1) read the engine's console (coverage, verification, manual actions, crawl errors) — minutes; 2) resubmit sitemap + request reindexing — minutes (what fixed it); 3) check infrastructure (robots.txt, DNS, certificate, headers changed by a deploy) — an hour; 4) only then consider content — weeks.
- **Lesson 4 — the bundling trap.** Rewrite pages + resubmit at the end → recovery → you credit the rewrite, it enters your playbook and charges you every time. Most "we rewrote and recovered" stories have an unmentioned resubmit. **Change one thing at a time, cheapest first, and write down what you expect before you do it.**

- **Concentration-risk exercise (10 min):** list each channel and its share of last month's users; for each, what happens if it returns zero for 30 days; what notice you'd get (search: none); for any channel over 50%, the one thing that still works on day 30. For them: brand queries (49.3%), bookmarks, referring domains — all built before an incident, none buildable during it. "A channel you did not build is not a channel you can deploy."

### Ch 23. Position 8 is a different game

### Ch 24. Be linkable, not promotable
- "Make great content and people will link" is unfalsifiable. Nobody links to a great document merger.
- Every unpaid referring domain (a large curated free-resources community on two high-authority domains, an independent tools directory, a maker platform's weekly feature) cited the **architecture** (local processing, nothing transmitted), never the tool list. 60 tools earned nothing; one architectural decision earned everything.
- **You're linked for being interesting, not useful.** A useful tool gives a writer no sentence; a checkable claim about how things could work gives them a paragraph. People repeat claims, not capabilities. The spreaders are the verifiers — a false interesting claim costs more than none.
- Ask: **"What is the most interesting true sentence about what I have built?"** Make it prominent, one-step verifiable, short enough to repeat. For technical founders it's rarely in the feature list; it's in the hard technical problem (described as engineering, not benefit), what your architecture allows that theirs forbids (ch 5), original data nobody else measured, or a defensible opinion on how the field should build.
- A product listing isn't a technical-community submission; the engineering is. They spent months pitching *what it does* to audiences who only cared *how it was built*.
- **Channels ranked by what worked:** 1) curated community lists/resource hubs (permanent, high-authority, free, need a genuine reason — every unpaid link came from here); 2) technical communities (pitch engineering, never product; usually one attempt — prepare); 3) comparison/alternative directories (list yourself against each incumbent; mechanical, durable); 4) launch platforms (spike + a few permanent links; once, done well); 5) direct outreach to ranking articles that already link to tools like yours (low hit rate, on-topic when it lands); 6) journalist-request services (lottery, cheap ticket).
- **Target to break out of 8–10: 20–30 new referring domains over three months (~2/week)** = 3–5 outreach emails/day + 2–3 genuine community contributions/week for a quarter.
- Anatomy of their three earned links: community free-tools list (accepted on a stated criterion — free, no account, no catch); independent tools directory (editorially chosen); maker platform Project of the Week (curator-selected). Three curators, all cited how it works.
- **Eight shapes of an interesting sentence:** "we never receive your data" + one-step verification · "runs with no server at all" + demo · "we solved [specific hard problem]" (e.g. OCR in WebAssembly, layout-preserving conversion, sub-second rendering of huge files) · "we measured something nobody has" · "it works offline" · "we deliberately didn't build X" (no accounts/tracking/upsell) with reasoning · a careful, fair opinion on what competitors get wrong · "we give away what others charge for, and here's why it's sustainable".
- **Test the sentence:** repeatable accurately after one reading? verifiable in one step? surprising to someone who knows the category? Fail any → keep working; it's your whole off-page strategy.
- No sentence? **Go measure something** — original data needs no permission or authority, only work (their example: test 50 document tools to see which upload your file — a browser, a network tab, an afternoon, a spreadsheet). Boring is why it works.
- Implication: architecture is a distribution decision. Between two viable architectures, ask which gives a stranger something to say.

### Ch 25. How to reach DR 20 in a weekend, and why it won't save you
- DR 0 → 25 in nine months. Tiers: **0 bookmark networks** (weekend; moves DR a little; doesn't move rankings; a risk) · **1 directories** (weekend; moves DR a lot; barely moves rankings) · **2 launch platforms** (a week of prep; a little, briefly) · **3 editorial** (months, indirect; moves DR a little; **moves rankings**).
- **Tier 0 — don't.** Several hundred auto-publishing "bookmark[word].com" sites; they did ~100. Devalued since the early 2010s; of 33 checked, 8 dead (no DNS/no response), others parked/repurposed; a link scheme by definition (no editorial placement, no humans); undermines a trust-based position (combined with the fake-rating incident it reads as a pattern); and their own data had already shown directory links don't move rankings.
- If you already did it: don't panic or rush to disavow (Google says most sites shouldn't; it ignores obvious spam; over-disavowing can remove helpful links; the tool is for manual actions or clear datable drops). Order: check **Manual Actions** in GSC (the only emergency trigger) → prepare a disavow file but don't submit → keep legitimate bookmarking tools out of it (Diigo, Pinboard, Raindrop, Pearltrees, Symbaloo, Wakelet) and real publishing platforms → redirect effort to tier 1 and tier 3.
- **Rule: before any link tactic, ask what it looks like to a person who checked** (someone deciding whether to cite you). Links that only make sense to a crawler do nothing and may cost you.
- **Tier 1 directory run:** several hundred SaaS/tool directories list anything, often in exchange for displaying their badge. They placed ~85; the footer carried 93 badge links (startupfa.me, twelve.tools, saasbadge.com, toolparade.com, open-launch.com…) until 14 Sep 2026.
  - Prep once (then ~3 min/submission): asset pack (square + wide logo at 512px+, 3–5 consistent screenshots, 30-second silent screen recording — half reject on image dimensions); copy at four lengths (60-char tagline, 160-char summary, 500-char description, 1,500-char long description); 3–5 categories reused everywhere; a dedicated muteable email alias (lists get sold); a tracking spreadsheet (directory, date, status, live URL, badge required). Submit in batches of ten; expect ~half live, ~quarter require badge, ~quarter never respond.
  - Result: DR 0 → 21; ~122 linking sites, ~1,300 backlinks (≈10 per site = a network repeating itself); only 61% of sites and 17% of backlinks pass equity. Then DR rose 21 → 25 over nine months with no new links (third-party revaluation) while rankings didn't move. **A metric that rises while you do nothing isn't measuring your work.**
  - **The dozen worth more than the other ninety:** Crunchbase, G2, Clutch, GoodFirms, DesignRush, Kompass, Manta, Hotfrog, BBB, CrowdReviews + **Google Business Profile and Bing Places** (entity registrations — the lever when a competitor has sitelinks and you don't). Twelve careful listings beat a hundred automated ones.
  - Badge-for-listing is a reciprocal exchange — flagged, not defended. Test: could a real human find you there and click through?
- **The reframing comparison:** them vs near-identical competitor — referring domains 103 vs 101; DR 21–25 vs **1.5**; competitor wins the shared brand query and has sitelinks. **DR is a vendor's model, not a Google metric. Directory links inflate the model without moving what it predicts** (Google discounts links every product gets automatically).
- What directories do buy: an indexed floor (your name returns more than your own site), entity consistency, a little referral traffic, social proof if you like badges. Not position. Budget one weekend, once. Costs: 93 outbound footer links; by September 73 third-party badge images loaded on the busiest page — removed (may cost some listings that require the badge; decide in advance how long a badge wall stays).
- **Tier 2:** Peerlist Project of the Week (also in sameAs with GitHub, dev.to, Medium) — best return per effort for technical makers; developer audience, thin competition, no following needed. Smaller engaged platforms beat big ones where you drown. Product Hunt (numbers not published): prep a week ahead (gallery, 60-char tagline, first comment explaining why you built it, a hunter if possible); first comment = the engineering story; launch Tue–Thu early in the platform's day; expect a spike that collapses within 72h — the durable value is the link and badge. Show HN (numbers not published): the engineering is the Show HN, not a tool listing; roughly one attempt (reposts noticed); the comments are the product — be present, answer technical questions properly, concede valid criticism; failure mode = pitching a product to people who want a build log.
- **Tier 3:** every chosen citation cited the architecture. No submission form — build something with a sentence (ch 24) and get it in front of the right communities.
- **Directories buy a number. Launch platforms buy a day. Editorial links buy positions.** Run directories once, then never look at DR again; spend the next three months on 20–30 editorial links.

### Ch 26. How to win a launch with nothing
- 12 Jan 2026: #1 on Peerlist Launchpad, winner of the week — at 19 days old, 49 changes shipped, 1.7 Google clicks/day (20 total in January to date), no audience/list/following. 229 upvotes, 38 comments, 14 bookmarks, 3,543 views; 4.94/5 from 16 ratings (Idea 4.94, Execution 4.88, Design 5.00).
- **You don't launch because you have traction; you launch to get the first traction.** A launch is upstream of traffic. It's the only channel with no settlement lag — search pays in 3–4 months, a launch pays this week.
- **Launch copy structure (reusable):** open on the reader's problem in a specific recognisable moment (late-night urgency), not the product (product not mentioned until paragraph five); name a specific villain (a made-up sketchy converter domain; a bloated desktop install) — sharper than "other tools"; include the honest "just give up" option (earns trust; two bad options alone reads as a setup); one-sentence turn that echoes the product name; then the claim in plain language — files never leave the browser, plus "I literally can't see them" (turns a promise into a structural fact).
- Public proof: a sceptical commenter checked the network tab, then turned off the internet, and reported it worked; another recorded an unprompted demo video and posted it to X. You can't manufacture that; you make it possible by choosing a claim that survives testing.
- The work: reply to essentially every comment (substantively — e.g. plain "no" on open-source, thanking the video maker, moving the sceptic's comment to testimonials); a launch page is a room, not a billboard. Four screenshots + logo (Design scored highest — on launch day screenshots are the product). State the stack plainly to technical audiences (React, PDF.js, Ghostscript WASM, PWA) — naming the hard part is the credential.
- Why Peerlist not the biggest platform: rank depends on the field (like ch 21); a first place where you can place beats mid-table where you can't. The win became a permanent editorial link.
- **Playbook:** launch before you feel ready · open on the reader's problem with a specific moment and villain · admit the give-up option · lead with one verifiable, repeatable claim · four consistent screenshots · name the hard technical part for technical audiences · block the day and answer every comment · pick a platform where you can win · **launch in month one, not month six**.

### Ch 27. Teardown: why the other guy wins
- ~2 hours, once a quarter, one competitor. Subject: near-identical name/concept on a different ccTLD — referring domains 103 vs 101, DR 25 vs 1.5, competitor wins the brand query and holds sitelinks.
- **Seven steps:**
  1. Find who actually competes: search your top 10 queries, record every domain that outranks you, sort by frequency (assumed rivals often absent; unknown sites appear six times).
  2. **Count referring domains, not DR**; then quality — editorial (chosen) vs directory (anyone can get). Their DR gap was entirely the directory play. **If your authority metric and your rankings disagree, believe the rankings.**
  3. Check the brand entity: search your brand — who ranks first, who has sitelinks, who owns a knowledge panel. View their homepage source: does the title lead with the human-typed brand or a domain string? Organization schema with alternateName and sameAs? (Competitor: spaced human brand in the title + sitelinks; them: domain string, no sitelinks → an entity gap worth up to 49.3% of query-attributed traffic.)
  4. Score their best-ranking page against the ch 7 spec (title length, direct answer, steps, FAQ count, depth sections, internal links, schema). Theirs better → fixable. **Theirs worse and still winning → content isn't the variable; stop editing.** (The common case past pos 8.)
  5. Diff query sets: theirs-not-yours = gap list; **yours-not-theirs = your moat** (usually the odd uncontested queries) — defend those before chasing theirs.
  6. Find their most-*linked* pages (not highest-traffic) and read them — shows what their audience found interesting. If none have editorial links, they bought their position and can't defend it — your opening.
  7. Finish with one sentence: "They beat me because ______." Can't fill it → not finished; more than one thing → not prioritised. Theirs: the search engine is more confident the competitor is the entity "I Hate PDF".
- Purpose: not tactics to copy — stopping you working on the wrong problem (an afternoon of this would have saved two rounds of on-page work).

### Ch 28. Fifty emails, two links
- 50 emails → 2 links (4% — a good rate).
- **Finding pre-qualified targets** (pages already linking to tools like yours) with operators: `"best free [category] tools" intitle:2026` · `"[your differentiator]" -[your brand]` · `"[incumbent] alternative" -[your brand]` · `"free [category]" "[objection you resolve]" inurl:blog`. Priority: niche blogs in your differentiator's community → "tools for [profession]" listicles → **university/institutional help pages** (link to free tools routinely, real authority, rarely pitched).
- **Email structure:** subject references their exact article title as "an addition"; open by naming their specific guide; one sentence on what your product does + the verifiable architectural claim + how to check it in one step (go offline after load); say which of *their* readers it serves (e.g. anyone handling sensitive files); free, no sign-up; one link; no attachment; ask for nothing beyond consideration; thank them either way. **One follow-up after 5–7 days, then stop permanently.**
- Rhythm: **3–5 emails/day + 2–3 genuine community contributions/week**; track in a sheet (target URL, contact, date sent, follow-up date, outcome). Judge on an **8–12 week lag** (February outreach shows up in April).
- Never buy links, run/join a PBN, post fake reviews, or spam comments. Grey area = public directory badge exchange (lists every product, a human can find you); a closed circle that exists only to pass equity is a penalty risk.
- **Expected reply distribution per 50:** 30–35 no reply · 8–12 polite declines (thank, move on, don't argue) · 3–6 "I'll take a look" (half become links) · 1–3 immediate yes · 0–2 asking for money (decline — that's buying a link). Two or three links per fifty is a good week (~5%); expecting 30% makes people quit in week two.
- **Objections:** "we don't add links to old posts" → ask if anything upcoming fits, then leave it. "How is this different from [incumbent]?" (best reply) → architectural difference + verification step in two sentences; if it needs a table you don't have a differentiator. "Is this yours?" → yes, immediately and plainly ("I built it, so I'm biased — here's the check you can run yourself").
- **Targets nobody pitches:** university/institutional help pages (library guides, IT help desks, student resource lists — `site:.edu "free pdf tools"` and equivalents) and niche community wikis/resource hubs (every earned link came from this category). Both reward meeting a stated criterion, not a pitch — read what they list and why, then say which criterion you meet.
- **Stop rules:** two emails per target max (a third converts nobody and costs reputation); **100 well-targeted emails with nothing = your sentence isn't interesting — fix it** rather than sending 100 more; never stop because it feels slow (judging in March what February started is the most common way link building "fails").
- 3–5 emails/day + 2–3 contributions/week for a quarter ≈ 20–30 referring domains in ~40 min/day. A daily habit survives the month where nothing lands; a batch gets abandoned.

## Part V — The bill

### Ch 29. Five expensive mistakes (ranked by cost)
1. **Starting link acquisition in month seven instead of month two.** Seven months on the working lever, nothing on the next one; links share the 1–4 month lag. The on-page ceiling was foreseeable by month two; 30 min/day of off-page work could have run in parallel. **Start the slow-compounding channel before you need it.**
2. **Ninety blog posts.** 94 indexed blog pages → 1,913 clicks (1.71%). They competed with (and lost to) product pages on the same queries and split signals (one post still cannibalises a tool page on a query worth thousands of impressions a week); topic intent browses, task intent converts. **The ten posts worth writing instead:** comparison posts "X vs Y" (evaluation intent, no cannibalisation, commercial value); one objection-handling explainer (best link magnet they had); original research; technical write-ups for engineers (who link). **Test before writing: could a product page rank for this query instead? If yes, build the product page.**
3. **Fabricated ratings.** Four pages carried AggregateRating 4.9 from 8,500 non-existent reviews for several weeks (rationalised at pos 8 with no clicks: "product is good, everyone does it"). Removed: violates structured-data guidelines, manual-penalty trigger that can cost site-wide rich results or the site. Worse: a trust-based product ("files never transmitted") can't sit on a page lying about something checkable. Kept the one real rating (4.8 from 124 responses). **With no budget, credibility is the only asset that compounds faster than content.** Define headline numbers precisely and state the definition ("250K users" = 249,472 unique users in Clarity, 25 Dec 2025–17 Sep 2026; rounded up by 528, i.e. under four hours of growth — stated in the body, not a footnote).
4. **~100 bookmark-network submissions** (see ch 25). Obsolete since early 2010s; a quarter dead; a link scheme; their own data already showed directory links didn't move rankings. No measurable cost yet (Google mostly ignores these) — that's not vindication; it's tail risk beside the fake ratings in a trust-selling profile. Response: check Manual Actions, prepare a disavow file, don't submit without cause, spend the next weekend on the dozen real directories.
5. **Geography and a four-minute domain choice** (.cv — Cape Verde, ~500k people — because the .com was taken). Markets: India 951,069 impr / 5.71% / 8.12 · Malaysia 123,209 / 3.33% / 8.48 · US 541,165 / 1.82% / 11.14 · UK 168,279 / 1.75% / 8.86 · Australia 89,790 / 1.42% / 9.34 · New Zealand 19,179 / 1.20% / 14.14 · Cape Verde 230 / 2.17% / 9.08. Gap narrowed (1–6 positions vs up to 10 in first edition). Can't separate ccTLD effect from competition (competition alone explains it). **Domain rules:** prefer .com, pay the premium; otherwise only extensions treated as generic through common use (verify first); never a country extension for a country you don't sell to; search the brand first; consider whether you can credibly take payment on it.
- **Kept unchanged:** one entry point per job (ch 6); cheap, numerous, unpredictable bets (ch 3); the architectural differentiator (ch 5); static delivery (ch 11); free, no account (26.92% task completion); a problem with existing demand (ch 1).
- Do: check Manual Actions in Search Console monthly.

### Ch 30. The playbook, and the problem it creates
- **The eight decisions:** 1) choose a problem people already search for; 2) count sub-problems before building (one job = landing page, sixty = business); 3) make each attempt cheap (5 of 227 URLs = 75%, unpredictable); 4) target queries nobody serves (two obscure conversions at 4.89/5.67 out-earned the head term 6.5× on half its impressions); 5) every job its own page, working immediately, no account; 6) start earning links in month two (on-page ceiling ~pos 8; links have ~3-month lead time); 7) don't confuse DR with authority (85 directories → DR 21 in a weekend; DR 1.5 competitor still wins); 8) read the dashboard as a report on last quarter. No ninth.
- **The problem:** 250K users, effectively no revenue. No account = no relationship/reach; no server = no metering/limit to lift; nothing transmitted = no data on users. Paths each cost something: an API contradicts the architecture (files go to a server — must be a separately, honestly positioned product); a paid tier is hard when free has no ceiling; merch is brand not revenue; monetise the audience *beside* the product (this book).
- **Decide how you'll charge before you decide how you'll grow** — growth architecture constrains revenue architecture expensively. Still: distribution is harder to manufacture than revenue.
- **Failure modes and tells:** 1) building for demand you must create — tell: can't write ten query strings without inventing (ch 1). 2) one product/page/lottery ticket — tell: decomposition under 20 entries (ch 6). 3) months polishing the "sure thing" — tell: average build over two days (ch 3). 4) a year on an unwinnable head term — tell: you sorted queries by volume (ch 4). 5) signup wall before trust — tell: a stranger can't complete anything without giving you something (26.92%). 6) seven months on-page, then the ceiling, then +3 months lead time — tell: you've never sent an outreach email (ch 29). 7) a weekend of directories, a satisfying number, no movement — tell: you know your DR but not your position on your top query. 8) changing strategy during the lag — tell: you checked yesterday and formed an opinion (316% daily swing).
- **Order matters:** 1–4 are decisions before code (expensive to reverse; get them wrong and 5–8 can't save you); 5 is the build (longest, most obviously productive, so people stay too long); **6 must overlap 5, not follow it** (30 min/day from month two); 7 and 8 are continuous disciplines.
- One sentence: **find demand you can reach, build the cheapest possible doors to it, start earning links before you think you need them, then wait a quarter before believing your dashboard.**
- Position 8 is the end of solo work; most founders read the wall as failure and restart (and hit the same wall a year later).

## Part VI — What came next

### Ch 31. The long tail ranks better and pays less
- By query length: **1–2 words** 112 queries / 33,729 clicks / 45.5% of clicks / 2.79% CTR / pos 7.79 · **3–4** 486 / 28,513 / 38.4% / 4.48% / 8.03 · **5–6** 278 / 7,588 / 10.2% / 5.38% / 7.15 · **7+** 124 / 4,330 / 5.8% / 6.34% / 6.89.
- 112 short queries out-earn 402 long ones 2.8× while ranking worse. Impressions: 1,207,366 (short) vs 209,322 (long) — you can win the long query and still be splitting a pool six times smaller. **Ranking ≠ earning.**
- Longer queries are easier to rank for (effect real but smaller than thought; brand inflates the two-word row — remove brand and the gradient returns).
- Length ≠ competitiveness: "merge pdf" (head), "pdf editor" (mid-tail), "ihatepdf" (navigation) are all short. Short is where volume is, and short isn't a synonym for unwinnable — ch 4 lost one head term, not the other 111.
- CTR now rises with length (2.79 → 6.34%), reversing the first edition (5.26/5.18/4.28/5.09%). The tail didn't improve — the short row got bigger and worse (brand + bottom-of-page-one head terms). **Query length predicts CTR only after removing brand and unwinnable head terms.** Near-miss long queries (rank well because nobody wrote them, convert poorly because the page only half answers) still exist.
- **Do:** don't treat length as a proxy for value (it partly proxies difficulty only); **sort your tail by clicks, not position** (position reports flatter it); don't build pages for queries you only half answer; **mine the tail for language, not demand** — it's a spec written by users, feed it into ch 7 titles; **multiply queries × impressions × realistic CTR before committing a quarter** (here 11,918 clicks over 267 days ≈ 45/day across 402 queries).
- The analyses you skip because you "know the answer" are the ones most worth running.

### Ch 32. Nobody has data on day one
- **For roughly the first 120 days, absence of signal is not evidence of anything.** You can't tell working from failing with traffic data in the first four months. Use binary instruments.
- **Four day-one questions:** 1) does it exist? `site:yourdomain.com/the-page` (not indexed ≠ indexed-not-ranking; see ch 22). 2) is what you wrote what a crawler sees? view source, not devtools. 3) does anyone search for it? autocomplete test (a wrong answer invalidates everything downstream). 4) is the query already answered well? read the top five — four thorough + one 2019 forum thread = a gap; five excellent = a market → ch 5 question. Three answerable before code.
- **Instrument before launch:** Search Console verified + sitemap (no backfill — the day you verify is day one); privacy-respecting analytics with events on *success* actions (completed the job, not visited the page); **a dated change log** (a text file, ~10 sec/week — turns future data from description into explanation).
- **Chain: indexed → impressions → position → clicks → completions.** Diagnose from the front: zero impressions = indexing; impressions at pos 40 = competitiveness; impressions at pos 8 with no clicks = snippet (fix this afternoon).
- No early signal distinguishes "too soon" from "wrong" — make the bet cheap and the review date fixed (four months out). On that date check the four binary questions, not traffic; still flat with all four good → look at authority (ch 23).

### Ch 33. Measuring the answer engines
- No Search Console equivalent exists for ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, Copilot (and likely never will — the product is answering without sending the click).
- **Three defects:** 1) citation without a click — real outcome (recall, later direct visits, word of mouth), unmeasurable in principle; 2) referrers lie or vanish (some pass, some strip, some redirect; behaviour changes silently — a QoQ drop may be their product change); 3) volumes small enough to be noise (a 40% WoW move = four visits).
- **Method:** build one combined referrer segment for assistant hosts — chatgpt.com, perplexity.ai, claude.ai, copilot.microsoft.com, gemini.google.com, and the .oai variants — and watch it as one line. It's a **floor, not a measurement**; always report it as a floor.
- Monthly: ask each assistant the **ten queries you most want to be named for**, fresh session with no history (personalisation/history will show you your own site — the easiest way to fool yourself), and record whether you appear. **Write the ten queries once and never change them** (a moving question set can't show a trend).
- Real signal: **direct traffic and branded queries in Search Console** (an instrument that works; brand was 49.3% of query-attributed clicks).
- Won't claim: can't attribute the brand share to AI (coincides with two blogging platforms, an active Reddit presence, 250K users remembering the name). Confident "AI is X% of growth" numbers are an experiment worth seeing or a guess. Never put an indefensible number on a slide.
- **Do:** build the referrer segment today (no backfill, ~10 min); run the ten-query check monthly and keep answers in a file (12 months of a fixed set = a dataset); judge the channel on brand search; don't restructure the site for it (clear, quotable, checkable writing serves humans equally; machine-only tactics bet on a system that changes monthly).

### Ch 34. What it costs to charge money
- **Three hidden costs:**
  1. **Hosting may forbid commercial use.** Vercel Hobby prohibits commercial use (licence term, not a limit) → Pro at $20/month ($240/year). Cloudflare Pages allows commercial use on free with 100,000 function requests/day — moving took an afternoon and removed their only recurring infra cost.
  2. **Free databases may sleep.** Supabase pauses free projects after 7 days without a request (data survives, project stops answering) — a paying buyer locked out in a quiet week is the worst failure for a paid product. Workarounds: a cron pinging the DB, or $25/month. Firebase Spark doesn't pause or need a card (39 chapter docs + one entitlement record per buyer is ~1000× inside the free allowance).
  3. **Your processor may not take your customers' money.** India = 48.54% of search traffic (54,298 clicks, 5.5× the US). Many developer-friendly platforms route through Stripe, card-only; UPI is roadmap-only. Card-only would have silently failed the biggest market and looked like poor conversion. **Check the processor supports how your largest market pays before checking fees.** (They use Dodo Payments — cards and UPI, tax handled at checkout.)
- **The hard part: a browser can't keep a secret.** First paywall checked a localStorage flag and hid chapters while shipping all 30 chapters in the JS bundle. Fix principle: **the text must not be in the file you send.** Build splits the manuscript: free chapters + a preview of each paid one are bundled; paid remainders go to a directory never imported or deployed, fetched at read time one request per chapter, served only if a **database security rule** allows it.
- **Two tests worth copying:** a **leak test** — search the built output for every withheld paragraph; fail the build if any appears (runs every build). A **rules test** — run DB security rules against a local emulator asserting signed-out visitor, signed-in non-buyer, and lapsed subscriber each get exactly what they should.
- Can't protect: screenshots (blur on capture-shortcut modifier keydown defeats common shortcuts; a phone camera defeats everything). What changes behaviour: **per-buyer watermark** (purchaser's email rendered into the page) — makes leaks attributable; must be disclosed in the privacy policy (undisclosed is indefensible and unlawful in several jurisdictions). Disabling right-click/selection/print = cheap friction against the casual only.
- **Do:** read host terms on commercial use before pricing; ask what the free tier does after a week idle; let your country report choose the processor (five minutes); move paid content out of the bundle first (provider-agnostic, the only load-bearing part); **write the leak test first** — without it you have an intention, not a paywall.

### Ch 35. The tools are the marketing
- RankHonestly gives away twelve free, no-account, browser-only tools: Search Console analyzer, whole-site SEO auditor, schema generator, schema inventory, SERP preview, position-value calculator, internal link graph, OG image generator, noise-or-signal test, programmatic set analyzer, crawler view, log file analyzer. They cost more to build than the marketing site.
- Logic: the ceiling is authority = referring domains; outreach converts ~2 per 50 emails; nobody links to a paid book, they link to what it made. Free layer earns links → domain authority → paid layer benefits.
- **Three constraints for linkable free tools:** useful without the paid product (a demo for a purchase is an ad, ads don't get linked); do something free alternatives don't (their OG generator shows the nine surfaces the image lands on — e.g. WhatsApp crops 1200×630 to a square, cutting 285px off each side); verifiable (client-side — "nothing uploaded" checkable in the network tab).
- **Public bet (~65% confidence):** within 12 months of launch the tools will produce more referring domains than outreach of comparable effort. Check: referring domains at 12 months vs the 2-per-50-emails baseline. Likely failure mode: links to the tools while the book stays invisible (mechanism works, application to a paid product doesn't).
- **Do:** build the free thing from the paid thing's offcuts (diagnostics the paid product already explains — coherent, affordable); never put the sales pitch inside the tool (mention the paid product once at the bottom after the tool has been useful; asking for email first makes it a landing page in costume); make what it *can't* know the honest, most quotable part; write predictions down before results.

### Ch 36. What survived the second look
- First edition: 224 days, 42,320 clicks, 1,228,913 impressions. This edition: 267 days, 111,872 clicks, 3,764,967 impressions. Re-ran Part III with the same script and definitions (left column = first edition's 5 Aug export re-run through this edition's script).
- **Scorecard (first → now → verdict):** top-5 pages' share 75.4% → 75.2% held · a month shipping nothing still grew +61% (May) → +140% (Aug) held, larger · organise cluster below the fold 0.22% @ 8.78 → 0.20% @ 8.63 held · brand share of query clicks 42.4% → 49.3% held, larger · CTR rises again past position 10, yes → yes held · tablet best device 6.59% @ 6.56 → 3.62% @ 8.03 **broke** · query length doesn't predict CTR, flat → 2.79→6.34% rising **broke** · collapse from pos 3–4 to 8–10: 8.6× → 14.6× worse · non-brand CTR 3.02% → 1.94% worse · authority vs on-page impressions 38:1 → 80:1 worse · US avg position 12.81 → 11.14 better.
- Five held, two broke, three worse, one better. **Structural findings survived; small-sample findings didn't.**
- Held: the power law didn't soften (67 more URLs earned traffic, clicks ~3×) — **plan for concentration, don't wait for it to pass**; settlement lag reproduced at triple scale (two instances months apart = a mechanism); negative results held hardest (organise didn't move while exposure tripled) — **re-check your non-movers, not just your wins** (wins are most likely luck).
- Broke: tablet (pure sample size — the rule written in advance said not to build on it); query length (the short row absorbed brand + newly-visible head terms like compress/merge pdf; short share 35.6% → 45.5%, CTR 4.38% → 2.79%). Corrected: length predicts CTR only after removing brand and unwinnable head terms.
- Worse: visibility tripled; **77.6% of non-brand impressions now sit at pos 6–10 converting at 1.02%**; 54 pages hold 2,750,220 impressions (73% of all) at 1.03% CTR — page-one-bottom pages needing authority.
- Better: US 12.81 → 11.14 (India 7.66 → 8.12). Appendix F prediction 3 (US +2 places by June 2027 if competition, not geography, explains it): 1.85 places of the way from 12.99, but referring domains haven't risen — if it comes true without links, the reasoning was wrong. DR 21 → 25 with backlinks flat (~1,300), rankings unmoved.
- **Two ways a re-run lies:** 1) comparing two definitions and calling it a trend (head-term impressions "200×" vs ~137× like-for-like) — if the computation changed, you can't difference against the old number. 2) tool limits mistaken for world changes (GSC's 1,000-row export cap pushed the 1–2-click tail off the file; looked like consolidation). Also Appendix A: Clarity's long export computes interaction rates over ~one day silently.
- **Habit:** keep old exports (free to store; the only way to difference later); **re-run when data doubles, not by calendar**; re-run the same script, not the same idea (from memory you'll redefine something); record which findings were thin when published (stated confidence makes collapse informative).

### Ch 37. Programmatic pages, and the 43% that do nothing
- 94 template-generated tool pages: **11 earned 88.6% of clicks**; 40 earned <10 in 267 days; 20 earned exactly zero.
- pSEO = many pages from one template + one data source, each targeting an existing query (page per city, currency pair, file conversion, integration). Sound when your product genuinely does many distinct jobs.
- Distribution (of 69,927 tool-page clicks): 1,000+ clicks: 11 pages / 61,978 / 88.6% · 100–999: 18 / 6,991 / 10.0% · 10–99: 25 / 883 / 1.3% · 1–9: 20 / 75 / 0.1% · 0: 20 / 0. **43% of pages = 0.1% of traffic.** Got more extreme with growth (earlier: 63 pages, 4 winners at 74.9%, 9 zeroes).
- Correction: count pages, not GSC rows — the same page appears once per hostname served (129 rows / 47 zeroes vs 94 pages / 20 zeroes). **Merge hostnames before counting.**
- Each page cost ~20 minutes (template existed); the 40 duds ≈ a day and a half total; pdf-to-audio (11,955 clicks) took the same 20 minutes. **It's a cost strategy: make attempts cheap enough that being wrong 44 times doesn't matter.**
- **The zeroes all had impressions** — indexed, served at ~pos 20+, nobody clicks. redact-pdf: 19,394 impr, 95 clicks, 0.49% at pos 11.58 (visible but unpersuasive). **Diagnostic: failing at pos ~30 → needs authority (a year); failing at pos ~12 with impressions → needs a better title (20 minutes).**
- **Where it goes wrong:** pages not genuinely different (only a noun varies = one page sixty times; test: does a reader get an answer specific to what they typed? — their pages each *do* the job); thin data source (a city page with no city-specific data is what spam filters target, and it indexes at first, which feels like success); generating before verifying demand (20 zeroes were knowable in an afternoon via ch 1 — fine at 60 pages, catastrophic at 10,000); scale (dead weight multiplies, crawl budget spent on unwanted pages).
- **How to make it work:** one query per page, verified in autocomplete / PAA / a forum before the page exists (the only reliable zero-killer); make the page do the job, not describe it (tool pages 2.04% CTR vs 0.82% for blog posts on the same topics); ship slowly enough to read feedback (sixty in a week tells you nothing); kill or improve dead pages — don't ignore the answer they already gave; **don't scale until one page from the template has proven itself.**
- Do: split zero-click pages by impressions (none = authority/demand problem; plenty = title problem).

### Ch 38. The page, assembled (the master checklist — start here when building)
Evidence grades: **Measured** (number in this dataset) · **Repaired** (a defect shipped and fixed — right to do, unmeasurable alone) · **Standard** (untested here; cheap, well-documented downside) · **Negative** (tested, did less than promised). Grades rate evidence, not importance.

**Layer 0 — before there is a page**
- Is anyone searching? List literal strings; can't list ten without inventing → stop. *Standard* (ch 1)
- Can you win it? At zero authority pick the job big sites don't serve well. *Measured* (head term 366,840 impr / 1,724 clicks / 0.47%; two unserved conversions 11,277 clicks at pos 4.9/5.7 on half the impressions)
- One page, one query. *Standard*

**Layer 1 — the site, once**
- Every URL serves finished HTML (title, description, headings, body, structured data in the served file). *Measured* — Appendix C rounds 1–2: 106 impressions (Dec) → 25,058 (Feb); the one very-high-confidence change.
- Tags survive page load — the app mustn't overwrite the server's title/description on mount. *Repaired* (the "meta-clobber" bug: correct static titles replaced by generic ones on mount, live for an unknown period, flagged by nothing).
- One hostname: pick www or not, 301 the other, absolute self-referencing canonicals. *Repaired*.
- Sitemap of only real canonical indexable URLs; robots blocks nothing wanted. *Standard*.
- Organization + WebSite on homepage with alternateName (how people type your name) and sameAs (profiles you really use). *Standard* (argued sitelinks lever).
- Responsiveness on real users: **INP < 200ms, CLS < 0.1, LCP < 2.5s**. *Measured as a cost after the click* (296ms INP, 0.126 CLS, 37.16% dead clicks); ranking effect not measured — CWV is a light ranking factor; fix speed for people.
- HTTPS everywhere, mobile-first, no interstitial over content on first paint. *Standard*.

**Layer 2 — the page, in build order**
1. Direct answer, 40–80 words right after H1, written first. *Standard*.
2. H1 = near-verbatim query + differentiator; one per page; differs from title. *Standard*.
3. FAQ 6–8 real questions (PAA, autocomplete, forums), visible. *Negative on the traffic promise* (15 question queries, 0.2% of non-brand visibility) — keep for AI synthesis and to surface objections.
4. Depth: 2–4 blocks (doing it without the usual downside; specific use cases; when to use something else); best pages 1,200–2,000 words. *Measured and Negative* — worst page 758 words; deepening six pages coincided with ~7,100 → ~12,300 clicks/28 days (moderate confidence) but they stayed in the 6–10 band. **Depth gets you onto page one; it doesn't move you up it.**
5. Steps 4–6 imperatives naming real buttons, visible. *Standard*.
6. Internal links 3–5 to siblings inside body prose, anchor = target's query. *Standard*.
7. Meta description < 155 chars, unique, mechanics + objections, no adjectives. *Standard*.
8. Title < 60 chars, query first, tail naming what the searcher fears. *Measured* (pos 6–10: 5.79% vs 0.71% — 8×; pos 3–6: 6.79% vs 5.32%; inference from searcher wording).
9. Working product usable without scrolling, account or banner. *Measured* (26.92% completion).
10. Head tags: absolute self-canonical, OG + Twitter mirroring title/description/URL, real 1200×630 share image, no stray noindex. *Standard + Repaired*.
- Pattern: Measured items are about **the click** (title, product); ranking items are Standard/Negative. On-page decides what a position is worth; something else decides the position.

**Layer 3 — structured data by page type** (doesn't move rank; changes result size/appearance)
- Homepage: Organization (alternateName, sameAs, logo) + WebSite. Never: blocks describing a single tool.
- Tool/product page: core type (WebApplication / SoftwareApplication / Product) with offers price, standalone BreadcrumbList, HowTo if it has steps, FAQPage if visible questions. Never: Organization (homepage only), AggregateRating without real ratings.
- Article/guide: Article or BlogPosting with author and real dates, standalone BreadcrumbList, FAQPage only if visible. Never: placeholder dateModified.
- Listing/index: BreadcrumbList, optionally ItemList. Never: markup for things not on the page.
- Rules: breadcrumb as its own top-level block (*Repaired*); everything marked up is visible; no rating without ratings (removing the fake 4.9 cost no measurable traffic — *Measured as absence of loss*); generate JSON with JSON.stringify from the same data as the page, absolute URLs.
- Verify on built output: Rich Results Test, Schema Markup Validator, GSC Enhancements, view-source (blocks in served file, not script-added).

**Layer 4 — after it ships**
- View source of the built page: title, description, H1, a body sentence present — every deploy. *Repaired* (the two worst defects passed checks that looked at the wrong layer).
- Open the live page and read the browser tab title — every deploy (served-file checks can't see a post-load overwrite). *Repaired*.
- Log the change with date and expected effect — every change. *Standard*.
- Judge after a full quarter — every change. *Measured* (1–4 month lag).
- Watch real sessions monthly: dead clicks, errors, completions. *Measured*.
- Run the toolkit pre-deploy checklist every deploy, including "CSS-only" ones.

**Annotated real page — /pdf-to-handwriting** (built late July 2026 to spec from the start): title 54 chars, query first, "Free" as the objection; description 146 chars; H1 near-verbatim query, differs from title; canonical absolute www self; five JSON-LD blocks (BreadcrumbList, Organization, WebApplication price 0 no rating, HowTo 4 steps, FAQPage 10 questions); ~1,809 words. Deviations: direct answer 97 words (over 40–80); 10 FAQs (over 6–8); Organization on a tool page (off-spec, harmless); prose internal links only 2 (to home/about) with siblings in a separate block (off-spec — want 3–5 siblings in prose); site-wide default share image (off-spec). Meets every *Measured* item; loose on four *Standard* ones.
- Result: page 3,727 clicks / 14,382 impr / **25.91% / pos 3.98**; head query "pdf to handwritten notes" 472 / 1,033 / 45.69% / 2.28. Site curve predicts 11.82% at pos 3–4 — runs >2× (partly because a pos-2.28 head query lifts the page blend).
- **Same spec, opposite result:** /organize-pages (repaired into spec and retitled) 8.63 / 0.20% — >100× CTR difference. And on the handwriting page, the crowded query "text to handwriting converter" sits at 9.49 / 4.19%. **What separates them is Layer 0** — layout-preserving PDF-to-handwriting was nearly unserved; page organising is served by every big PDF site.
- **The spec decides whether you're eligible and what your position is worth; it doesn't decide your position. Where supply is thin decides that, then authority.** The list stops you losing; what you point it at is how you win.

**THE CARD (summary):** before the page — one query, real demand, a job big sites don't serve well · once for the site — finished HTML on every URL, one hostname, absolute canonicals, clean sitemap, Organization + WebSite on homepage, real-user speed under thresholds · the page, backwards — direct answer, H1, FAQ, depth, steps, prose links, description, title last · markup — core type with a price, standalone breadcrumb, HowTo and FAQPage only for visible content, no rating without ratings, generated not typed · after shipping — read served source and live tab, date the change, wait a quarter, watch real sessions.

### B. The five-day diagnostic (product, no traffic — run first)
- **Day 1 Demand:** 20 literal phrases → autocomplete → discard invented → fewer than 10 survive = stop and reconsider (ch 1).
- **Day 2 Supply:** for each, judge the weakest page-one result; mark "takeable" or not; sort by takeable, not volume (ch 4).
- **Day 3 Decomposition:** smallest complete jobs for a cold stranger, aim 20+; estimate build time; average >2 days → redesign (ch 3, 6).
- **Day 4 Differentiator:** most interesting true sentence; repeatable after one reading and verifiable in one step (ch 5, 24).
- **Day 5 Instrumentation:** search + behavioural analytics; define the single activation event; calendar reminder four months out: "do not judge the strategy before this date" (ch 14, 18).
- Caveat: one case — mechanisms general, hit rates not.

### C. The change log — seven rounds, and what each one moved
- Attribution is structurally hard: settlement lag overlaps rounds shipped less than a quarter apart; nobody changes one thing at a time; the baseline moves (updates, competitors, seasonality; May grew 61% doing nothing). Honest unit: "directionally correct, here's the evidence".
- **Rounds 1–2 Foundations** (unique title + meta per route, canonicals, static generation, sitemap/robots, core schema). Before: every route the same shell — "one page wearing sixty hats". After: 106 impressions (Dec) → 25,058 (Feb) → 1,341,728 (first 17 days of Sep). **Confidence very high.** Lesson: the biggest jump is fixing why you're invisible — check this before anything (toolkit Prompt 3 automates it).
- **Rounds 3–5 Depth, schema, speed** (six highest-impression tool pages to full ch 7 spec; HowTo + FAQPage; bundle split, es2020 target, separate React vendor chunk). Clicks ~7,100 → ~12,300 per 28 days (windows ending 8 Jun and 23 Jul). **Confidence moderate** — overlaps March's payout, only 6 of 60 pages touched, impression base expanding. Clean negative: deepened pages stayed in the 6–10 band. Depth necessary, not sufficient.
- **Round 6 Silent bugs:** 6a standalone BreadcrumbList (nested version largely ignored; 20 minutes). **6b meta-clobber:** static generator wrote correct unique titles, then React components overwrote them with a generic title on mount — served HTML perfect, live DOM wrong; every served-file check passed; live for an unknown period; nothing flagged it. 6c non-www canonicals on four tool components on a www site. **High confidence they were correct; unmeasurable.** Lesson: **SEO bugs don't announce themselves; the traffic you never got leaves no trace. Only a mechanical pre-deploy check on the built output AND the live DOM, every deploy, catches them.**
- **Round 7 Removing things:** fake AggregateRating removed from four components; /organize-pages retitled to lead with "Organize" (~92% of cluster demand). Organise cluster still 269,773 impr / 0.20% / pos 8.63. **High confidence, negative result** — at pos ~8.4 the snippet isn't the constraint. Removing a liability is worth it with no measurable gain.
- Scoreboard: four rounds → one unambiguous win, one ambiguous improvement, one invisible repair, one clean negative. Normal, and never published — case-study "we fixed titles here" annotations are drawn after the fact on the nearest change.
- **Change-log rules:** date every change with what you expect it to do; change one class of thing per week; wait a full quarter before scoring; record negative results (more reliable — failure to move isn't explained away by lag); never annotate a chart after the fact.

### E. Glossary (book's definitions, condensed)
Activation event — the moment a stranger gets the value (here a completed download, 26.92%); define before instrumenting · Aggregate rating — fabricating it is a policy violation and manual-action trigger · Anchor text — the target page's primary query · Authority — not directly measurable; approximated better by counting editorially given referring domains than by vendor scores · Canonical — absolute, www-consistent · ccTLD — treated as geographic except a handful used generically (.co, .io, .me, .ai); **not overridable in Search Console** · CLS — >0.1 needs work · CTR — interpretable only segmented and within a position band · Direct answer — 40–80 words after H1; snippet and AI-citation candidate · Disavow — only for a manual action or a datable drop · DR — one vendor's model; manufacturable · Editorial link — the only kind that reliably moves position · Entity recognition — produces sitelinks; Organization schema, alternateName, sameAs, consistent naming · Featured snippet — lifted from the most self-contained dense passage · Head term — most contested, usually worthless at low authority · hreflang — single language: en + x-default and stop · Impression — at 8+ mostly someone choosing a competitor with you on screen · INP — <200ms good · JSON-LD — script block in head generated from page data · Long tail — by-product of depth (here past top 100 = 21.2%) · Manual action — check monthly · Average position — hides its distribution · Programmatic SEO — asset if each page fully answers its query, spam if templated and thin · Referring domain — distinct linking site; the count that matters · Rich result — more screen, not higher position · Settlement lag — 1–4 months · Sitelinks — for entities the engine is confident about; not requestable · Speakable — unclear benefit · Static generation — recommended shape for acquisition surfaces · Wasted visibility — high impressions, near-zero clicks; best targets.
